Slip Sheet Lip and Tab Design: Choosing the Right Pull Configuration

Corrugatedslipsheet Editorial • November 2026

The lip, or pull tab, is the single most operationally important feature of a slip sheet, yet it is the detail most often specified incorrectly. The tab is the flap that extends beyond the load footprint and that a push-pull attachment grips to move the sheet and its load onto or off a pallet or forklift platen. Getting tab count, dimension, and placement right determines whether your material-handling operation runs smoothly or jams repeatedly. This reference walks procurement and warehouse engineers through the decision.

How Push-Pull Handling Works

A push-pull attachment mounts to a forklift in place of forks. Its gripper jaw clamps the slip sheet tab, and a platen retracts the loaded sheet onto the truck. To deposit, the platen pushes the load off while the sheet stays behind or travels with it. The tab is the only interface between the machine and the load, so its dimensions must match the gripper's jaw opening and reach.

Tab Configurations Explained

  • Single-tab (one-sided pull): A tab on one edge only. Simplest and lowest material cost. Best for operations that always approach the load from the same orientation.
  • Two-tab (two-sided): Tabs on opposite or adjacent edges, allowing pull from two directions. Common in cross-dock operations with variable approach angles.
  • Three-tab: Flexibility for multi-directional handling in tight staging areas.
  • Four-tab (four-sided): Tabs on all edges for maximum handling flexibility and often used where loads transfer multiple times. Highest material cost but lowest handling-error rate.

For PP sheet stock, these configurations are available in PP corrugated slip sheets cut to your pallet footprint with the tab geometry built into the die.

Tab Dimension Specifications

A tab that is too short slips the gripper; too long and it fouls adjacent loads or racking. Key dimensions to lock:

  • Tab length (overhang): commonly 2 to 4 inches beyond the load edge, matched to your gripper reach.
  • Tab width: full edge width or a centered partial tab, depending on gripper jaw span.
  • Underhang vs overhang: the sheet should slightly underhang the load footprint on non-tab edges to avoid catching during transfer.

Gauge and Coefficient of Friction

Tab performance interacts with sheet gauge. A heavier gsm sheet resists tearing at the tab under pull load but requires a gripper rated for that thickness. The sheet's surface coefficient of friction also matters: too slick and the load slides during transit; too grippy and the push-pull stalls. Specifying a textured or treated top surface with a slick bottom is a common solution. These variables are configurable when ordering custom corrugated plastic sheets sized to your exact load and equipment.

Matching Tabs to Your Equipment

Before finalizing any slip sheet order, document your push-pull attachment's make, model, jaw opening, and platen width. Share these with the manufacturer so tab geometry is engineered to the equipment rather than guessed. A mismatch here is the leading cause of slip sheet program failure and the reason some operations wrongly conclude slip sheets do not work for them.

Bottom Line

Choose tab count by how many directions you handle loads, size tabs to your specific gripper, and match gauge and surface friction to load weight and stability needs. Specify to the equipment, not to a generic standard, and your slip sheet program will deliver the labor and freight savings it promises.
