Push-Pull Attachment and Slip Sheet Selection Guide

Corrugatedslipsheet Editorial • October 2026

The Attachment Drives the Specification

Operations teams often select a slip sheet first and discover equipment compatibility problems later. The correct sequence is the reverse: the push-pull attachment on your forklift—its design, condition, and operator skill—should drive the slip sheet specification, not the other way around. A sheet that performs flawlessly with one attachment type can tear, slip, or jam with another. Understanding this relationship up front prevents the most common and frustrating failures in a slip sheet program.

Two Dominant Attachment Styles

Most warehouse fleets run one of two attachment types, and each places different demands on the sheet.

  • Fork-mounted push-pull: Mounts to standard forks and uses a gripper bar to clamp the sheet's lip and pull the load onto a platen. It is versatile and lets the truck double as a conventional forklift, but it applies concentrated gripping force to the lip, demanding tear-resistant lip reinforcement.
  • Integral (dedicated) push-pull: Replaces the forks entirely with a platen and gripper assembly. It handles loads faster and more gently but commits the truck to slip sheet work only. Its smoother pull reduces lip stress, allowing slightly lighter gauge sheets in some applications.

Before writing a sheet spec, document which attachment style each truck carries, the gripper bar width, and the platen length relative to your load footprint.

Lip Design and Gauge Matching

The lip is where attachment and sheet meet, and it is the most failure-prone element. The gripper must seize enough lip to pull the full load without the material tearing away. For fork-mounted systems with their concentrated grip, specify a longer lip and a gauge with proven tear resistance along the grip line. Elipacko's PP corrugated slip sheets are engineered with reinforced lip geometry sized to common gripper bar widths, which reduces the lip-tear failures that plague thinner single-use fiber sheets.

Single-Lip, Multi-Lip, and Four-Sided

Lip configuration should match your handling pattern. A single-lip sheet is pulled from one side only—simple and economical for straightforward flows. Two-lip and four-sided designs allow pulling from multiple directions, which matters when loads are accessed from different angles in racking or staging. Each added lip raises material cost slightly but can dramatically improve throughput where directional flexibility is needed. Map your actual pick and put-away directions before defaulting to a four-sided sheet you may not need.

Gauge, Load Rating, and Durability

Attachment type informs gauge, but load weight and trip count finalize it. Heavier loads and higher column stacks require thicker twin-wall board to resist deflection during the pull. For reusable closed-loop programs, durability across wash cycles and repeated grips is paramount—a sheet that survives 50 trips transforms the cost-per-trip math versus disposable fiber. Specify both the static load rating and the expected trip life, and ask the vendor for grip-cycle test data, not just a single-pull rating.

Operator Technique and Program Success

Even a perfectly specified sheet fails under poor technique. Train operators to seat the gripper fully on the lip, pull at a controlled speed, and avoid shock loads that concentrate stress. A brief certification process pays for itself in reduced sheet damage and fewer dropped loads. Pair the equipment and sheet spec with a short SOP, and audit technique periodically.
